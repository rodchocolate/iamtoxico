The jazz canon, often referred to as the Great American Songbook, is a loosely defined collection of significant 20th-century American jazz standards, popular songs, and show tunes. It comprises influential American popular songs and jazz standards from the early 20th century that have endured in their legacy.

definition and scope

The Great American Songbook is not a specific list of songs or a book, but a canon of enduring tunes from the 1920s to the 1950s. These songs were created for Hollywood musical film, musical theatre, and Broadway theatre. Culture writer Martin Chilton defines the term as tunes from Tin Pan Alley—the music publishers' row on New York's West 28th Street—as well as Broadway and Hollywood movie musicals. According to Chilton, these songs became the core repertoire of jazz musicians between roughly 1920 and 1960. Radio personality Jonathan Schwartz and singer Tony Bennett have described the genre as "America's classical music".

key figures and analysis

The canon includes standards by songwriters such as George Gershwin, Cole Porter, Irving Berlin, Jerome Kern, Harold Arlen, Richard Rodgers, Oscar Hammerstein II, Lorenz Hart, Hoagy Carmichael, and Johnny Mercer. In a 1972 study, Alec Wilder, a songwriter and critic, listed and ranked artists he believed belonged to the canon, emphasizing the analysis of composers and their creative efforts in the work titled American Popular Song: The Great Innovators, 1900–1950.

performances and revivals

Ella Fitzgerald, an American jazz vocalist known as the "First Lady of Song," recorded interpretations of the Great American Songbook with Verve Records. Other artists have revived these songs in albums; Ringo Starr released Sentimental Journey in 1970, featuring songs from the 1920s, 1930s, and 1940s. Pop singers from the 1960s or later, such as Bob Dylan, Lady Gaga, and Rod Stewart, have also released albums reviving songs from the canon. Linda Ronstadt's 1983 album What's New, recorded with Nelson Riddle, was described by Stephen Holden of The New York Times as the best and most serious attempt to rehabilitate an Idea of pop that was undone by the mass marketing of rock LPs and Beatlemania in the mid-'60s.
